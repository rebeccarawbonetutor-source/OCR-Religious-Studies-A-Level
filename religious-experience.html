Philosophy of Religion: Religious Experience

Religious Experience
Knowledge & Understanding

What is a religious experience?

Definitions

Direct experience of the divine, its nature and its influence.

Definition & Schleiermacher

  • Directly experienced: not learned second-hand from scripture or tradition.
  • William James ("The Varieties of Religious Experience", 1902): "The feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine."
  • Schleiermacher ("On Religion", 1799; "The Christian Faith", 1821): religion is neither knowledge nor morality but a feeling of absolute dependence — the self wholly contingent on a greater power.

Nature and influence

  • Nature: quasi-perceptual awareness of the divine — the person experiences, rather than reasons to, God.
  • Paul: persecutor become apostle to the Gentiles; three missionary journeys, much of the New Testament.
  • Teresa of Avila: reformed the Carmelite order; shaped Christian mysticism for centuries.
  • James: judge experiences "by their fruits", not their roots — the moral change they produce.

Mystical and conversion experience

Types

Stace, Teresa of Avila, James's divided self and the Damascus road.

Mystical experience

  • Definition: a direct feeling of union with, or awareness of, a greater power, beyond ordinary sense experience.
  • Stace ("Mysticism and Philosophy", 1960): extrovertive — unity perceived through the world; introvertive — unity found within, the ego dissolving in stillness and prayer.
  • Teresa of Avila ("The Autobiography"): visions, locutions (inner words from God), raptures; four degrees of prayer symbolised by the "four waters", from laborious effort to passive union.
  • The transverberation: an angel pierces her heart with a flaming golden dart (ch. 29), leaving her "on fire" with love of God — sculpted by Bernini, "The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa" (Rome, 1645–52).

Conversion experience

  • Definition: a transformative turning towards God, changing beliefs, values and way of life.
  • James: the unification of a divided self by a power beyond conscious control — once-born (gradual, "healthy-minded") vs twice-born (sudden crisis of the "sick soul", the profounder type); worked up from the subliminal self.
  • Paul, Damascus road (Acts 9.3–9; also 22 and 26): blinding light, "Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?", three days' blindness healed by Ananias; Paul reads it as God choosing "to reveal his Son in me" (Galatians 1.16).

Two key thinkers

Scholars

Otto on the numinous; James on the four marks of mysticism.

Rudolf Otto — the numinous

  • "The Idea of the Holy" (1917/1923): the heart of religion is the numinous — the "wholly other", beyond concepts and words.
  • Mysterium tremendum et fascinans: awe and dread together with fascination drawing one in.
  • Creature-feeling: one's own nothingness, "mere dust and ashes", before an overwhelming power.
  • A priori: a category the mind brings, not learned from the senses.
  • Non-rational, not irrational: later "schematisation" dresses it in rational ideas (father, love), which the raw experience outruns.

William James — four marks and conclusion

  • Ineffability — defies expression.
  • Noetic quality — feels like insight into truth.
  • Transiency — brief, though recallable (lectures 9–10).
  • Passivity — grasped by a power beyond control.
  • Conclusion (lectures 16–20): authoritative for the one who has them; suggest a wider self — a "more" with which we are in touch — but cannot prove its nature to others.

How can experiences be understood?

Interpretations

Union, psychological effect, or physiological effect.

The three readings

  • As union with a greater power: genuine contact — Schleiermacher (dependence), Otto (the numinous), Teresa (union in prayer), James (the wider self) — evidence of the transcendent.
  • As a psychological effect: an illusion produced by the mind (Feuerbach, Freud, Jung) — real to the person, but psychic, not divine, in origin.
  • As a physiological effect: the product of body and brain — epilepsy, drugs, fasting and sensory deprivation produce "religious" sensations.

Psychology and physiology

Challenges

Feuerbach, Freud, Jung and the physiological evidence.

Feuerbach — projection

  • "The Essence of Christianity" (1841): God is the projection of idealised human qualities onto a heavenly father; "theology is anthropology".
  • Religious experience is an encounter with our own projected ideals.

Freud — illusion and wish fulfilment

  • Religion is "the universal obsessional neurosis of humanity" ("Obsessive Actions and Religious Practices", 1907).
  • "The Future of an Illusion" (1927): an illusion derives from wishes, not evidence — God the projected father-figure of helpless humanity.
  • "Civilization and Its Discontents" (1930): the "oceanic feeling" traced to the infant's undissolved ego, not to God.

Jung — the collective unconscious

  • "Psychology and Religion" (1938): religion is the experience of the numinosum, an agency not under conscious control.
  • Its source: the collective unconscious — universal archetypes, including the God-image.
  • Unlike Freud: the experience is psychologically real and vital — but its origin is the psyche, not necessarily God.

The physiological evidence

  • Temporal lobe epilepsy: ecstatic auras — Dostoevsky described such an aura before his seizures.
  • Persinger's "God helmet": induced a "sensed presence" by magnetic stimulation — contested, not always replicated.
  • Newberg ("Why God Won't Go Away", 2001): imaging of praying nuns and meditating monks shows changed blood flow during intense practice.

Corporate (shared) experiences

Examples

Experiences shared by a whole group at once.

Pentecost and the Toronto Blessing

  • Pentecost (Acts 2): the Spirit as tongues of fire; all speak in other languages and the crowd hears its own tongues — the model corporate experience.
  • The Toronto Blessing (1994, Toronto Airport church): congregations affected together — laughter, weeping, shaking, falling — spread worldwide by report and expectation.
Analysis & Evaluation

The argument from religious experience

The Argument

Swinburne, Alston and the cumulative case.

Swinburne — two principles

  • "The Existence of God" (1979).
  • Principle of Credulity: "in the absence of special considerations, if it seems to a subject that x is present, then probably x is present" — if it seems God is present, He probably is; the burden of proof lies with the sceptic.
  • Principle of Testimony: others' experiences are probably as they sincerely report them.
  • Rejecting religious experience wholesale would demand rejecting sense experience too.

Alston — Perceiving God

  • "Perceiving God" (1991): religious experience is a doxastic practice — a socially established way of forming beliefs, like sense perception.
  • It is rational to trust it unless shown unreliable; there is no neutral standpoint that discredits it without discrediting perception too.

The cumulative case

  • No single experience proves God, but millions of experiences across every culture and era, consistently reporting a greater power, together make theism probable.
  • A posteriori, inductive — a probability argument, not a proof.

The three debate questions

Debates

Testimony, corporate experiences, and the basis for belief.

Is personal testimony enough to support validity?

  • Yes: Swinburne's Principle of Testimony — we trust sincere reports everywhere else; rejecting them here is arbitrary.
  • James: the fruits corroborate sincerity — Paul's transformed life is not the behaviour of a fraud; Teresa's self-critical accounts (doubting her own visions) show an honest witness.
  • No: sincerity is not truth — people sincerely report alien abductions; whole communities report incompatible gods.
  • Key point: testimony transfers the experience, never its interpretation — "it seemed to me to be God" is all it can deliver.

Are corporate experiences more reliable?

  • Yes: many witnesses at once corroborate each other; a shared hallucination by independent observers is far harder to accept than one delusion; corporate experiences found communities — the Church grew from Pentecost.
  • No: crowd psychology — expectation, contagion, suggestion; the Toronto Blessing spread by anticipation of its effects — evidence of suggestion, not the Spirit. Group agreement shows shared psychology, not a shared object.

The conflicting claims problem

  • Christians experience the Trinity; Muslims Allah; Hindus Brahman — incompatible objects.
  • Not all can be veridical: most experiencers must be mistaken — so why trust any?
  • Reply: all agree on one greater power — Hick's "the Real" beyond all human images; disagreement about the object's nature is not evidence against its existence.

Further challenges

Objections

Projection, verification and Ockham's razor.

Projection and wish fulfilment

  • Feuerbach: God is projected ideals — the experience is an encounter with ourselves.
  • Freud: the mind's wish for a protective father — an illusion by definition, from wishes not evidence.
  • Both give a complete alternative explanation needing no God — so the experience cannot be evidence for one.

The verification challenge

  • Ayer ("Language, Truth and Logic", 1936): meaningful only if verifiable — a private, ineffable, transient experience cannot be checked, repeated or shared; never public evidence.
  • Reply: other minds and the past are unverifiable too; Ayer's criterion famously fails its own test.

The physiological challenge

  • Every reported feature can be artificially produced — epilepsy, the "God helmet", imaging, fasting, drugs.
  • If the brain generates the content alone, God is an unnecessary extra — Ockham's razor cuts it out.

Evaluation

Evaluation

Strengths and weaknesses of the argument.

Strengths of the argument

  • The fruits (James): transformed lives — Paul, Teresa — are historical facts; effects verifiable even if objects are not; authority for the experient cannot be stripped by argument.
  • Epistemic fairness (Swinburne, Alston): the sceptic's standard of doubt, applied consistently, destroys perception, memory and testimony; we check perception only by more perception.
  • The critics concede the phenomenon: Jung — psychologically real and vital; Freud concedes the feeling; the dispute is over its cause, not its reality.
  • Non-competing explanations: a natural mechanism does not exclude God as cause — how it occurs never settles whether it is divine contact; the circuitry explains the music, not the absence of an orchestra.
  • Conflicting claims survivable: accident witnesses disagree wildly — perception is partial, not empty; Hick's Real preserves one object behind many images.
  • The right kind of claim: probability (Swinburne), authority for the experient (James) — as a cumulative strand the argument carries real weight.

Weaknesses of the argument

  • Conflicting claims: if most experiencers are sincerely wrong, "seems to me" to "is" is shown unreliable in this very field.
  • A complete rival explanation: projection and wish fulfilment explain the phenomena without remainder — Ockham's razor cuts out the needless extra.
  • Symmetric physiological evidence: everything can be induced; the receiver reply — brain as receiver, not generator — is an assumption, unfalsifiable.
  • Privacy defeats public proof: ineffability, transiency, passivity — James's own marks — make outside assessment impossible; never above probability for the non-experient.
  • Receiver reply is a defence, not evidence: "God could work through the brain" assumes what it needs to show.
  • Probability is thin: it cannot convert a sceptic — the evidence is always second-hand.
